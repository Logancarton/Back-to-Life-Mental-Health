# Back to Life Mental Health Photography Plan

This file is the source of truth for new photography used on the public website. The goal is a small, coherent image library that feels like Back to Life Mental Health rather than a generic behavioral-health stock site.

## Photography direction

Use real practice/provider/office photography whenever possible. Supplemental Arizona environment photography should feel local, calm, warm, and natural rather than tourist-oriented.

Avoid:

- Generic sad-person or hands-on-head mental-health stock photos.
- Exaggerated clinical poses, white coats, clipboards, or staged handshakes.
- Visible patient information, charts, computer screens, prescription labels, or other PHI.
- Images that imply a service, staff member, office feature, or credential that is not actually part of the practice.
- Heavy filters, artificial HDR, obvious AI imagery, or inconsistent color grading.

Preferred visual qualities:

- Natural window light.
- Warm neutral backgrounds.
- Sage, cream, terracotta, wood, and dark-teal tones where naturally present.
- Candid or lightly directed expressions rather than formal headshots.
- Negative space for responsive crops.
- A consistent warm-neutral color grade across the full library.

## File standards

Store final web images in `assets/images/`.

Preferred delivery:

- JPEG or WebP for photography.
- sRGB color profile.
- Long edge approximately 1800-2400 px for major images.
- Keep original high-resolution files outside the website repo if desired; commit optimized web copies here.
- Target roughly 200-500 KB for most web photographs where quality allows.
- Use lowercase, hyphenated filenames.

Do not overwrite a photo with a materially different subject while retaining the same filename unless every crop has been rechecked.

## Priority 1 — photographs that change the website most

### 1. Homepage hero

Filename: `provider-hero.jpg`

Location: Homepage hero.

Shot: Logan seated naturally in the actual practice environment, body angled slightly toward camera, relaxed expression, hands visible. A conversational rather than formal portrait.

Composition: Landscape. Leave usable negative space around the subject so desktop and mobile crops both work. Do not crop tightly around the head or hands.

Target ratio: 16:9 source, with enough room for portrait-shaped responsive crops.

Why: This is the highest-visibility image on the website and should immediately establish that the visitor is seeing the actual provider and practice.

### 2. Homepage secondary provider image

Filename: `provider-office-candid.jpg`

Location: Homepage "Meet your provider" section.

Shot: Different from the hero. Logan working, listening, or seated informally in the office; not looking directly at the camera is fine.

Target ratio: 6:5 or 4:3.

Why: Prevents the current provider image from being repeated and makes the homepage feel professionally photographed rather than templated.

### 3. Medication-management visit

Filename: `medication-management-conversation.jpg`

Location: Medication Management page.

Shot: Logan seated in the provider chair across from an empty patient chair or photographed from the patient-side perspective. Notebook or tablet is fine if no PHI is visible. The visual should communicate listening and explanation rather than "prescribing."

Target ratio: 4:3 landscape.

### 4. Telehealth

Filename: `telehealth-provider.jpg`

Location: Telehealth page.

Shot: Logan at a laptop in a clean professional setting, camera near eye level. Screen content should be blank, abstract, or otherwise free of patient information.

### 5. Office wide shot

Filename: `office-treatment-room.jpg`

Shot: Clean wide view of the actual treatment room showing both seating positions and the atmosphere patients will encounter.

## Condition-page image library

Condition pages should use metaphorical or ordinary-life imagery rather than literal symptom stereotypes. One strong image per page is enough.

### Anxiety

Filename: `condition-anxiety.jpg`

Idea: Open desert trail or person walking through a wide landscape with visible space ahead. Calm, grounded, not fearful.

### Depression

Filename: `condition-depression.jpg`

Idea: Natural morning light entering a quiet room or an outdoor scene moving from shade into warmer light. Avoid lonely-person clichés.

### ADHD

Filename: `condition-adhd.jpg`

Idea: Realistic active workspace with notebook, calendar, a few simultaneous tasks, and visual structure. Engaged rather than chaotic.

### PTSD

Filename: `condition-ptsd.jpg`

Idea: Shaded foreground opening into a wider safe landscape, or another subtle transition from enclosure to space. Do not depict traumatic events.

### OCD

Filename: `condition-ocd.jpg`

Idea: Ordinary home/work environment with subtle visual rhythm or repetition. Avoid handwashing, checking locks, or other stereotyped compulsions.

### Bipolar disorder

Filename: `condition-bipolar.jpg`

Idea: Arizona landscape with natural transitions between light and shadow. Keep the metaphor subtle; do not imply split personality or dramatic emotional extremes.

### Grief & loss

Filename: `condition-grief-loss.jpg`

Idea: Empty bench, quiet path, or open landscape at warm late-day light. Reflective rather than bleak.

### Life transitions

Filename: `condition-life-transitions.jpg`

Idea: Trail curve, fork, changing horizon, doorway, or another natural transition image. Avoid obvious road-sign stock imagery.

Preferred condition ratio: 16:9 or 3:2 landscape, composed so center and side crops remain usable.

## Optional detail photography

These are useful after the priority images are complete:

- `office-detail-chair.jpg` — patient chair / room detail.
- `office-detail-desk.jpg` — clean desk, notebook, plant, or texture; no PHI.
- `office-detail-signage.jpg` — branded practice signage if present.
- `provider-explaining.jpg` — Logan mid-conversation/gesturing naturally.
- `provider-desk.jpg` — Logan working at desk with no readable clinical data.

These can be used as secondary images, section backgrounds, or social media crops without forcing additional stock imagery into the site.

## Placement rules

1. Do not put a large photograph in every section. White space is part of the design.
2. Use no more than 2-3 major photographs on a typical page.
3. Do not repeat the same provider portrait twice on the same page once the full photo library exists.
4. Use actual `<img>`/`<picture>` elements for meaningful page photography; reserve CSS backgrounds for decorative imagery.
5. Every meaningful image gets accurate alt text describing what is visibly relevant, not keyword stuffing.
6. Decorative texture/background images should use empty alt text or CSS so screen readers do not receive redundant descriptions.
7. Set explicit dimensions or aspect ratios to minimize layout shift.
8. Lazy-load images below the initial viewport. Keep the homepage hero eager/high-priority.
9. Verify mobile crops manually before publishing.
10. Keep condition imagery respectful and non-stigmatizing.

## Integration order

When new photos are available, integrate in this order:

1. Replace the current homepage hero source with `provider-hero.jpg`.
2. Replace the repeated homepage provider poster with `provider-office-candid.jpg`.
3. Add the Medication Management and Telehealth images.
4. Build the Contact wayfinding gallery from the three arrival images.
5. Add About/team/office photography.
6. Add condition images as a consistent collection.
7. Generate a dedicated social-sharing crop after the hero photography is final.

Do not change clinical facts, insurance, pricing, services, credentials, or public staff roles merely to fit photography.
