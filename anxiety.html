Anxiety

Anxiety can range from persistent background worry to panic, physical tension, racing thoughts, sleep disruption, and avoidance. Treatment starts by understanding the pattern, what may be driving it, and how much it is interfering with daily life.

Common signs

What it can look like

  • Excessive or difficult-to-control worry
  • Feeling keyed up, restless, or on edge
  • Muscle tension, headaches, or stomach symptoms
  • Trouble falling or staying asleep
  • Irritability or difficulty concentrating
  • Panic symptoms or avoidance of situations because of anxiety
Evaluation

Understanding the whole picture

Assessment looks at the type of anxiety, how long it has been present, triggers, physical symptoms, sleep, caffeine or substance use, medical factors, trauma history, medications, and the effect on school, work, relationships, and daily functioning.

Symptoms can overlap with other mental health conditions, sleep problems, medical conditions, substance use, medications, and major life stressors. Evaluation is used to sort through those possibilities rather than assuming one symptom equals one diagnosis.

Treatment

Care is individualized

Medication options

When medication is appropriate, options are selected based on the anxiety pattern, other symptoms, medical history, previous response, side-effect concerns, and patient preference.

Therapy support

CBT, exposure-based approaches, ACT, mindfulness-based work, and other evidence-based therapies may be useful depending on the anxiety pattern.

Daily systems

Sleep, caffeine, movement, routines, breathing skills, and stress-management strategies can influence the intensity of anxiety and are considered alongside formal treatment.

Frequently asked questions

Questions patients often ask

Does anxiety always require medication?

No. Some people improve with therapy, behavioral changes, or addressing an underlying contributor. Medication may be one part of treatment when symptoms are persistent or significantly impairing.

Can anxiety cause physical symptoms?

Yes. Anxiety can affect heart rate, breathing, muscle tension, the gastrointestinal system, sleep, and other body systems. New or concerning physical symptoms still need appropriate medical evaluation.

Can ADHD, depression, or trauma look like anxiety?

Yes. These conditions can overlap, and chronic stress from untreated ADHD or depression may also produce significant anxiety. A careful history helps separate the patterns.
